# Scene logic

Before any art is ordered, see the scene in your head. Every role answers for this, because a beautiful drawing that makes no sense breaks the film.

## The Writer

- **The end gives a clear answer.** A viewer must be able to say what happened and why it matters. A mystery may leave a question open, but only one, and it must be the right one.
- **Every hook is paid off,** and everything the ending needs has been shown before it.
- **Test it:** tell the story aloud in one sentence from the shots alone. If you need the script to explain it, change the story.

## The Director

Write `scene_logic` for every shot in `shots.json`, before art:

- **Where:** the place, and where people and objects stand within it.
- **Light:** where it comes from, and what is in darkness. In a dark scene, decide what is hidden and when it is revealed.
- **Before and after:** what is true when the shot starts and when it ends, and how the next shot continues from it.
- **What to show, and when:** the single thing the audience must look at, and what they must not see yet.

Vary the shots: wide, close, over the shoulder, low, high, moving. Use the camera for feeling. A film of side-on medium shots is not cinematic.

## The Artist

Look at the user's own art and approved samples first. Then, for each picture, ask:

- **Would this happen in real life?** Would a person keep a thousand paper boats in a heap on the floor, or neatly on a table and shelves?
- **Do sizes agree?** The same object stays the same size, and things shrink only with distance.
- **Is it where the story says?** A boat for the river goes on the water, not on sand.
- **Did the model add anything?** Remove extra people and props you did not ask for.
- **What is in front of what?** A person on a bridge stands behind its near railing, and a person on a road stands on it. A cut-out placed on top of a picture looks pasted. Put a separate foreground layer over the character, with the near railing, door frame, table or grass, and put their feet where they would really touch.
- **Do not draw what you do not need.** If a moment is hard to draw right, frame it out: show the hands letting go, then cut to the boat on the water.

## The Animator

Check every action before ordering its sheets:

- **Contact:** feet on the ground, hands really holding, objects resting on something.
- **Weight and gravity:** dropped things fall, cloth and hair follow, a flame leans with the air.
- **Cause and effect:** a knock shakes the door, a loud sound makes her flinch, and the sound lands on the drawing that causes it.
- **Direction:** a person running right means the world slides left. Moving things keep their direction across a cut.
- **Continuity:** the same hand holds the same object, in the same place, across shots.
- **Enough drawings:** see [smoothness](../../sumi-animator/references/smoothness.md).
- **The body and continuity:** see [action, physics and acting](../../sumi-animator/references/action-and-acting.md).
- **Show the cause before it matters:** if the wind "turns" later, show it blowing before. If something rises, it must keep rising on screen, even far away.
- **Show time passing:** a caption alone does not make ten years feel like ten years. Put one thing in the picture that says so.
- **Geography holds:** if two people are on opposite banks, show how one crosses, or stage the meeting so nobody asks.
- **End on a breath:** after the answer, give the audience a moment and a change of angle. Do not stop dead on the last line.
- **Say it once:** if the words are on the lantern, do not repeat them in a caption.
